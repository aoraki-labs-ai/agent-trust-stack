The previous six chapters covered what this field is doing. This one covers what it cannot do.
**The largest risk here is not that the technology is hard; it is that the narrative has run far
ahead of delivery.**

## Terms used in this chapter

| Term | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| **Sybil attack** | One party forging many identities to manufacture the appearance of consensus |
| **Deterministic memory encryption** | The same plaintext always encrypts to the same ciphertext, so it can be compared and replayed |
| **Freshness protection** | Preventing an attacker from replaying an old but legitimate ciphertext as the current value |
| **Ciphertext Hiding** | An AMD mechanism intended to mitigate ciphertext-comparison attacks |
| **Attestation signing key** | The private key a chip uses to sign attestation reports; leak it and any attestation can be forged |

ERC-8004 is the crypto ecosystem's affirmative answer to trustworthy agents: three registries for
identity, reputation and validation. <Claim id="erc8004-mainnet" full />

But this is the decisive one:

<Claim id="erc8004-validation-registry-absent" full />

**The Validation Registry is the only one of the three that carries cryptographic and TEE-backed
proofs.** It is not live, which means the whole system's notion of reputation currently rests on
**client feedback that costs nothing to forge**. Less than one cent rewrites the score of an agent
handling hundreds of dollars.

Put differently: of well over a hundred thousand on-chain agent identities, the genuinely verifiable
portion is zero. That does not make the direction wrong. It means the system is at the stage where
**registration is free so everyone registered**, and is a full order of magnitude of engineering away
from anyone depending on it for a decision.

## 7.2 The hardware root of trust has been hit directly

"Hardware root of trust" sounds immovable. Two rounds of work show it is not:

<Claim id="teefail-ddr5" full />

Within hours of disclosure, several decentralised projects depending on TEEs issued emergency updates
and migration plans.

### But the threat model has to be stated, or this misleads

All of these attacks require **physical access to the memory bus**. That makes the security of two
deployment shapes genuinely different:

- Inside a major cloud's data centre, the threat model is "the provider's staff or supply chain" — a
  high bar, legally constrained, physically guarded, and manageable.
- In a **decentralised TEE network**, the node operator is the party being defended against, and
  operators have **complete physical access to their own machines by construction** — which is exactly
  the precondition these attacks need.

Same silicon, same attestation report, radically different security depending on deployment shape.
This is the question the decentralised-confidential-computing narrative has to answer directly, not
wave past with "we use TEEs too".

<div class="falsify">
<strong>How to falsify</strong>
If the next platform generation introduces memory encryption with freshness protection — a full
Merkle tree or per-line counters — and a third party reproduces the attack failing against it, then
"physical attack is a structural problem for decentralised TEEs" downgrades to "a problem with one
hardware generation". Executable check: follow tee.fail's mitigations page and both silicon vendors'
security advisory numbers.
</div>

## 7.3 Zero data retention is a contract, not cryptography

Both frontier model vendors strengthened their enterprise confidentiality offers in 2026:

<Claim id="openai-private-safety-processing" full />

<Claim id="anthropic-zdr-retention" full />

The decisive fact: **neither published implementation detail.** Outside commentary broadly hopes a
correctly configured TEE sits behind this, but there is currently no evidence a third party can check.

Two different things are worth separating:

| | Contractual promise | Cryptographic or hardware guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| Rests on | The other party's willingness and legal exposure | The other party's capability boundary |
| Can an insider bypass it | Yes, at a cost | No, or only via physical attack |
| Can you verify it yourself | No, only pursue it afterwards | Yes, through remote attestation |

**Zero data retention sits in the left column.** It is not false, but it is a different thing from "the
provider is cryptographically unable to read your data". That gap is precisely the market for
confidential computing, and it is why Apple's move in section 2.6 of
[chapter 02](/agent-trust-stack/en/02-demand/) matters: in this market, the only thing that upgrades
"trust me" into "check for yourself" is remote attestation.

<div class="falsify">
<strong>How to falsify</strong>
If either vendor publishes an attestation procedure customers can verify independently — measurements,
a signature chain, tooling — then "a promise is not verification" stops being their shortcoming and
this section needs rewriting. Executable check: whether their enterprise privacy and trust centre
pages gain an attestation section.
</div>
